*Nitnem* means daily practice — the set of banis a Sikh reads each day. It is usually kept in three
parts across the day, and the app follows that same rhythm.

## The banis by time of day

In the **morning**, before or after Amrit Vela, the banis read are Japji Sahib, Jaap Sahib,
Tav-Prasad Savaiye, Chaupai Sahib and Anand Sahib. Anand Sahib, by Guru Amar Das Ji, opens:

The grouping of Nitnem into morning, evening and night banis follows long-standing, widely accepted
Sikh practice; the exact set can vary a little between traditions. The two lines above are shown to

## How the app helps

Gurbani Soul keeps your place in each bani, so you can put the phone down and pick up where you left
off. It keeps a quiet record of the days you have read, without scoring you or turning it into a
streak to defend.

Reminders are entirely optional and you set them yourself — a gentle nudge at the times you choose,
never a stream of notifications. Nothing about your reading leaves the device: there is no account
and no tracking.

Nitnem is a practice built on returning, not on finishing. Read at your own pace: a few lines with
attention are worth more than a page rushed through, and the app is designed to wait for you rather
than hurry you along. The banis themselves are unchanged whether you read one or all of them, so
there is no wrong place to begin.

If you want to read the full text of any bani, or look up a line you half-remember, the
[reader](/reader) and [search](/search) cover the whole Granth, Ang by Ang, in verbatim Gurmukhi.
