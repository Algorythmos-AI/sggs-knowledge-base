## The watches of the day

Alongside raag, Indian classical tradition ties melodies to times of day. The day and night are
divided into eight *pahars* — watches of roughly three hours each. Certain raags are associated with
certain watches, so that a raag sung at its proper hour is felt to sit most naturally.

## The Raag Clock

Gurbani Soul's Raag Clock brings these two ideas together. It shows the eight pahars around a dial
and, for the current watch, the raags associated with it. Times follow your device's clock, and you
can switch on a solar mode that follows sunrise and sunset where you are.

The deep-night watch is deliberately left quiet, rather than filled in — an absence shown as content,
not a gap. The Raag Clock is a way to notice the shape of the day, not a rule about when anything
must be read.

The raag arrangement also tells you something about how the Granth was compiled. Rather than group
compositions by author or by theme, the Gurus and Bhagats' words are gathered under the raag they are
sung in, so voices from different centuries sit side by side within one melody. Reading a raag from
its start therefore moves you through many hands at once, held together by a single musical setting.
